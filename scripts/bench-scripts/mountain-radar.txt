Mount Sinai Was Scanned With Powerful New Radar — What They Found Is Terrifying
Something underneath Mount Sinai should not exist. A joint radar survey conducted in two thousand and twenty three by a consortium of geophysicists, archaeologists, and remote sensing specialists mapped the interior of the mountain with ground-penetrating radar technology powerful enough to resolve objects the size of a human fist at depths of sixty meters. They expected to find geology. Rock strata, fault lines, the ancient granite bones of one of the world's most studied mountains. What the radar returned was something the project lead described, in a private email later shared with a colleague, as incompatible with natural formation. That email circulated. The survey data is now at the center of a dispute that the institutions involved would very much prefer not to be having publicly.
What the Survey Actually Found
This mountain has actually been scanned before. Let us be clear about that. Ground-penetrating radar surveys of significant archaeological sites are not unusual, and Mount Sinai, or the peak in the southern Sinai Peninsula currently identified with that name, Jebel Musa, has received various forms of remote sensing attention over the decades. But the two thousand and twenty three survey was different in two specific ways. The resolution was significantly higher than anything previously applied to this site, using a phased array radar system developed for deep geological survey in the mining industry and adapted for archaeological application. And the survey covered not just the summit plateau and the approach trails, but the full interior volume of the mountain to a depth that previous surveys had not reached.
Three distinct anomaly categories came back unexpectedly. The first was expected, variations in rock density consistent with the natural fracture patterns of ancient granite under centuries of thermal cycling. Nothing interesting there. The second category was more unusual, linear arrangements of subsurface density variations at relatively shallow depths, consistent with either natural dike intrusions or, potentially, worked stone. These linear features run in directions that do not align with the mountain's known geological structure, which is the detail that attracted attention. But it is the third category that generated the private email, the institutional dispute, and this script.
