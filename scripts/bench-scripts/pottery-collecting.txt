Ugly Old Pottery That Could Secretly Be Worth a Fortune
A woman in Portland paid four dollars for a chipped ceramic mug at a church sale last October. She almost left it on the table. Two months later a collector paid her nineteen hundred dollars for it. The mug was not pretty. A dull glaze, a hairline crack near the handle, nothing anyone would put on a shelf.
Between the nineteen twenties and the late nineteen fifties, a handful of American potteries were mixing their own glazes by hand and firing in coal kilns that no modern studio can afford to run. Every piece was thrown on a wheel and trimmed by hand. The kiln was loaded and watched through the night by someone who knew what the flame was doing.
Then the potteries were bought. Small family kilns were folded into larger ceramics groups. Investors wanted volume, so the wheels were replaced with slip-casting moulds and the coal kilns with electric tunnels. The stamp on the base of the mug stayed the same. What was underneath it did not.
A first-period piece in honest condition sells for anywhere between six hundred and four thousand dollars at auction. The later production goes for eight dollars at the same table. The mark on the base is the only thing that tells you which one you are holding. Check it before you wash it, before you price it, and before you agree to anything at somebody's kitchen table.
